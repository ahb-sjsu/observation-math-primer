\chaptermark{Spectral perturbation}

Every matrix the programme diagonalizes is an estimate. A read operator comes
from a blind probe that averages finite-difference gradients. A covariance comes
from a finite sample. A graph Laplacian comes from a finite neighbourhood graph.
The eigenvalues and eigenvectors that the papers report are those of the
estimate, and the question this chapter answers is how far they can sit from
the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix the estimate stands for.

The answer has two halves, and the title states both. Eigenvalues of a symmetric
matrix are stable. A symmetric perturbation of operator norm $\varepsilon$ moves
every eigenvalue by at most $\varepsilon$, whatever the symmetric matrix. Eigenvectors are stable only relative
to the distance between neighbouring eigenvalues, the \emph{eigengap}. A
perturbation of size $\varepsilon$ can turn an eigenvector by an angle of about
$\varepsilon/\text{gap}$, and when the gap is zero the eigenvector is not a
well-defined object at all. The chapter then measures the distance between two
subspaces by principal angles and by the overlap that the programme's probe
reports, states the Davis--Kahan theorem that turns ``noise over gap'' into a
bound, applies it to sample covariances, and ends with how spectra are computed
in practice.

The chapter builds on \cref{ch01}, which proves the spectral theorem, the
Courant--Fischer theorem and the facts about orthogonal projectors used here,
and on Primer~L, sections~L.7 and L.8 of \emph{Data Mining as Observation},
which introduce eigenvectors, power iteration and the singular value
decomposition. Throughout, $A$ and $E$ are symmetric $d\times d$ matrices, the
eigenvalues are listed in decreasing order $\lambda_1(A)\ge\dots\ge\lambda_d(A)$,
and two norms are used,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:norms}
\begin{gathered}
\norm{E}_2=\max_{\norm{x}=1}\norm{Ex}=\max_i\abs{\lambda_i(E)},\qquad
\norm{E}_F^2=\sum_{i,j}E_{ij}^2=\sum_i\lambda_i(E)^2,\\
\norm{E}_2\le\norm{E}_F\le\sqrt d\,\norm{E}_2 .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The first is the operator norm, the largest stretch $E$ applies to a unit
vector. The second is the Frobenius norm of Primer~L, section~L.8. Every number
in the examples, exercise answers and figures is recomputed by
\texttt{checks/ch13\_examples.py}.

\ompnexthead{Weyl's inequality}
\section{Each eigenvalue moves by no more than the size of the perturbation}\label{sec:ch13:weyl}

Start with the good news. Adding a symmetric perturbation $E$ to a symmetric
matrix $A$ can move its eigenvalues, but not far. The bound uses nothing about
$A$, neither its size nor its eigenvectors.

\begin{theorem}[Weyl's inequality]\label{thm:ch13:weyl}
For symmetric $A$ and $E$ and every $i=1,\dots,d$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:weyl}
\lambda_i(A)+\lambda_d(E)\ \le\ \lambda_i(A+E)\ \le\ \lambda_i(A)+\lambda_1(E),
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\abs{\lambda_i(A+E)-\lambda_i(A)}\le\norm{E}_2 .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For every unit vector $x$, the Rayleigh bounds of \cref{ch01} give
$x\T Ex\le\lambda_1(E)$, so $x\T(A+E)x\le x\T Ax+\lambda_1(E)$. The
Courant--Fischer theorem of \cref{ch01} writes $\lambda_i$ as a maximum over
$i$-dimensional subspaces of a minimum of the Rayleigh quotient. An inequality
that holds for every $x$ survives the minimum over each subspace and then the
maximum over subspaces, so $\lambda_i(A+E)\le\lambda_i(A)+\lambda_1(E)$. The
lower bound follows the same way from $x\T Ex\ge\lambda_d(E)$.
\end{proof}

The inequality is usually credited to Weyl's 1912 paper on the asymptotic
distribution of eigenvalues \cite{weyl1912}, the same paper that proved the
eigenvalue-counting law now also called Weyl's law. Horn and Johnson give the
general form $\lambda_{i+j-1}(A+E)\le\lambda_i(A)+\lambda_j(E)$
\cite{hornjohnson2012}. In words, \cref{eq:ch13:weyl} says that each eigenvalue,
viewed as a function of the symmetric matrix, is Lipschitz with constant one in
the operator norm. A Frobenius-norm version controls all the eigenvalues at once.
It is the Hoffman--Wielandt inequality \cite{hornjohnson2012},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:hw}
\sum_{i=1}^d\big(\lambda_i(A+E)-\lambda_i(A)\big)^2\le\norm{E}_F^2 .
\end{equation}

Weyl's inequality says how far an eigenvalue can move. For a small perturbation
of a \emph{simple} eigenvalue, one with no other eigenvalue equal to it, a Taylor
expansion says how far it does move. With $v_1,\dots,v_d$ the orthonormal
eigenvectors of $A$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:eigexp}
\lambda_i(A+tE)=\lambda_i+t\,v_i\T Ev_i+t^2\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{(v_j\T Ev_i)^2}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}+O(t^3).
\end{equation}
The first-order term is the Rayleigh quotient of the perturbation along the old
eigenvector. The second-order term already has the differences
$\lambda_i-\lambda_j$ in its denominators. Those differences rule everything
about eigenvectors in the next section.

\begin{example}[A two-by-two perturbation, three ways]\label{ex:ch13:weyl}
Let $A=\diag(3,1)$ and
$E=\begin{pmatrix}0.1&0.2\\0.2&-0.1\end{pmatrix}$. The perturbation has trace
$0$ and determinant $-0.05$, so its eigenvalues are $\pm\sqrt{0.05}=\pm0.2236$
and $\norm{E}_2=0.2236$. The sum
$A+E=\begin{pmatrix}3.1&0.2\\0.2&0.9\end{pmatrix}$ has trace $4$ and determinant
$2.79-0.04=2.75$, so its eigenvalues are $2\pm\sqrt{4-2.75}=2\pm\sqrt{1.25}$,
that is $3.1180$ and $0.8820$. Each moved by $0.1180$, inside Weyl's bound of
$0.2236$. The squared moves add to $0.0279$, below $\norm{E}_F^2=0.01+0.04+0.04+0.01=0.1$,
as \cref{eq:ch13:hw} requires. The first-order terms of \cref{eq:ch13:eigexp}
are the diagonal entries $3.1$ and $0.9$. The second-order terms are
$\pm0.2^2/(3-1)=\pm0.02$, giving $3.12$ and $0.88$, each within $0.002$ of the
exact values.
\end{example}

The symmetry of both matrices is essential. The nonsymmetric matrix
$\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\ \varepsilon&0\end{pmatrix}$ is a perturbation of size
$\varepsilon$ of a matrix whose eigenvalues are both $0$, and its eigenvalues are
$\pm\sqrt\varepsilon$. For $\varepsilon=10^{-4}$ they are $\pm0.01$, a hundred
times the perturbation. Nothing like \cref{eq:ch13:weyl} holds for general
matrices, which is one more reason the programme works with symmetric read
operators and covariances. Part of the bound survives when only $A$ is
symmetric. By the Bauer--Fike theorem every eigenvalue of $A+E$, for any $E$,
then lies within $\norm{E}_2$ of \emph{some} eigenvalue of $A$
\cite{hornjohnson2012}, but the eigenvalues may become complex and the pairing
by index is lost.

\Cref{fig:ch13:crossing} shows Weyl's inequality and its limit in one picture.
The path $A(t)=\diag(t,-t)$ has eigenvalues $\pm\abs t$, which cross at $t=0$.
Adding $E=\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ with
$\varepsilon=0.1$ gives eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt{t^2+\varepsilon^2}$. They never
touch, and they never stray more than $\varepsilon$ from $\pm\abs t$. The right
panel tracks the top eigenvector. Far from the crossing it hardly moves. Near
$t=0$ it turns through almost $90^\circ$ over a window of width about
$\varepsilon$, and for smaller $\varepsilon$ the turn is faster. With
$\varepsilon=0$ the top eigenvector jumps from $e_2$ to $e_1$ at $t=0$.
Eigenvalues are continuous functions of a symmetric matrix. Eigenvectors are not
continuous at a crossing. Near a matrix whose eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ is simple,
the unit eigenvector $v_i$, with its sign fixed, is a continuous and even
smooth function of the matrix.

\ompnexthead{Eigenvector sensitivity}
\section{An eigenvector turns by the perturbation divided by the eigengap}\label{sec:ch13:turn}

The second-order term of \cref{eq:ch13:eigexp} warned that eigenvalue
differences sit in denominators. For eigenvectors they appear at first order.
The cleanest place to see it is a $2\times2$ matrix, where the turn can be
computed exactly.

Let $A=\diag(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)$ with eigengap $g=\lambda_1-\lambda_2>0$, and
perturb it off the diagonal by $E=\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.
Subtracting the mean eigenvalue $m=(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)/2$, which changes no
eigenvector, leaves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:rot}
A+E-mI=\begin{pmatrix}g/2&\varepsilon\\ \varepsilon&-g/2\end{pmatrix}
=\rho\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta&\sin2\theta\\ \sin2\theta&-\cos2\theta\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \rho=\sqrt{g^2/4+\varepsilon^2},\qquad \tan2\theta=\frac{2\varepsilon}{g}.
\end{equation}
The matrix on the right is $\rho$ times a reflection across the line at angle
$\theta$, and that line is its eigenvector with the eigenvalue $+\rho$. So the
top eigenvector of $A+E$ is $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\T$, turned by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:theta}
\theta=\tfrac12\arctan\frac{2\varepsilon}{g}\ \approx\ \frac{\varepsilon}{g}\quad\text{for }\varepsilon\ll g .
\end{equation}
The angle depends on $\varepsilon$ and $g$ only through their ratio. The
eigenvalues meanwhile are $m\pm\rho$, each within $\varepsilon$ of
$\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, as Weyl requires. A perturbation that is small
compared with the eigenvalues can still be large compared with their
difference, and then the eigenvector turns up to its ceiling of $45^\circ$. That
ceiling belongs to this off-diagonal family. A diagonal perturbation can
reverse the order of the eigenvalues, and then the top eigenvector turns by
$90^\circ$. Adding $\diag(0,0.2)$ to $\diag(2.01,1.99)$ gives $\diag(2.01,2.19)$,
whose top eigenvector is $e_2$.

\begin{example}[Same push, two gaps]\label{ex:ch13:turn}
Take $\varepsilon=0.2$. With $A=\diag(3,1)$ the gap is $2$, so
$\tan2\theta=0.2$ and $\theta=5.65^\circ$. The first-order value $\varepsilon/g=0.1$
radians is $5.73^\circ$. The eigenvalues move to $2\pm\sqrt{1.04}$, that is
$3.0198$ and $0.9802$. With $A=\diag(2.01,1.99)$ the gap is $0.02$, so
$\tan2\theta=20$ and $\theta=43.57^\circ$. The eigenvalues move to
$2\pm\sqrt{0.0001+0.04}$, that is $2.2002$ and $1.7998$, by less than $0.2$ as
before. The same perturbation leaves the first eigenvector almost in place and
turns the second almost as far as it can go.
\end{example}

gaps. The initial slope of each curve is $1/g$ radians per unit of
$\varepsilon$, which is the $1/\text{gap}$ blow-up. The curves for small gaps
reach a large angle almost at once and then saturate.

For a general symmetric matrix the same picture holds direction by direction.
Differentiating $(A+tE)v_i(t)=\lambda_i(t)v_i(t)$ at $t=0$ gives
$Ev_i+Av_i'=\lambda_i'v_i+\lambda_iv_i'$. Taking the inner product with $v_j$ for
$j\ne i$ and using $v_j\T A=\lambda_jv_j\T$ gives
$v_j\T Ev_i=(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)\,v_j\T v_i'$, and taking it with $v_i$ gives
$\lambda_i'=v_i\T Ev_i$, the first-order term of \cref{eq:ch13:eigexp}. Keeping the
eigenvector at unit length makes $v_i\T v_i'=0$. So, for a simple eigenvalue,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:vecexp}
v_i(t)=v_i+t\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{v_j\T Ev_i}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}\,v_j+O(t^2).
\end{equation}
Each other eigenvector $v_j$ is mixed in with weight equal to the coupling
$v_j\T Ev_i$ divided by the eigengap $\lambda_i-\lambda_j$. When the couplings
are comparable, the nearest eigenvalue dominates. When $\lambda_j=\lambda_i$ the formula breaks down, which is
the crossing of \cref{fig:ch13:crossing}.

\begin{example}[Each direction pays its own gap]\label{ex:ch13:vecexp}
Let $A=\diag(3,2,0)$ and let $E$ have $E_{12}=E_{21}=0.02$, $E_{13}=E_{31}=0.03$
and zeros elsewhere. By \cref{eq:ch13:vecexp} the top eigenvector moves from $e_1$
to about $e_1+\tfrac{0.02}{3-2}e_2+\tfrac{0.03}{3-0}e_3=(1,0.02,0.01)\T$. The
smaller coupling produces the larger turn, because it acts across the smaller
gap. The exact eigenvector, scaled to first entry $1$, is
$(1,0.01999,0.01000)\T$.
\end{example}

\ompnexthead{Principal angles and overlap}
\section{Principal angles measure the distance between subspaces, and a random subspace overlaps a fixed one by $r/d$ on average}\label{sec:ch13:angles}

Turned eigenvectors come in groups. A probe estimates a read subspace of
dimension $r$, not a single vector, and two subspaces of the same dimension can
agree along some directions and disagree along others. One angle is not enough
to describe that. A list of angles is.

\begin{definition}[Principal angles]\label{def:ch13:angles}
Let $\mathcal R$ and $\hat{\mathcal R}$ be $r$-dimensional subspaces of $\R^d$ with
orthonormal bases $Q,\hat Q\in\R^{d\times r}$. The \emph{principal angles}
$0\le\phi_1\le\dots\le\phi_r\le\pi/2$ are defined by the singular values of
$Q\T\hat Q$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:angles}
Q\T\hat Q=U\,\diag(\cos\phi_1,\dots,\cos\phi_r)\,W\T,\qquad
\sin\Theta=\diag(\sin\phi_1,\dots,\sin\phi_r).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Changing either basis multiplies $Q\T\hat Q$ by an orthogonal matrix on one side,
which leaves the singular values alone, so the angles belong to the subspaces.
The first principal angle is the smallest angle between any vector of
$\mathcal R$ and any vector of $\hat{\mathcal R}$, and each later angle is the
smallest available after removing the pairs already used
\cite{golubvanloan2013}. The orthogonal projectors $\Pi=QQ\T$ and
$\hat\Pi=\hat Q\hat Q\T$ of \cref{ch01} turn the angles into three norms,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:projid}
\begin{gathered}
\tr(\Pi\hat\Pi)=\norm{Q\T\hat Q}_F^2=\sum_{i=1}^r\cos^2\phi_i,\qquad
\norm{\Pi-\hat\Pi}_F^2=2\sum_{i=1}^r\sin^2\phi_i=2\norm{\sin\Theta}_F^2,\\
\norm{\Pi-\hat\Pi}_2=\sin\phi_r .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The first identity is the cyclic rule,
$\tr(QQ\T\hat Q\hat Q\T)=\tr\big((Q\T\hat Q)(Q\T\hat Q)\T\big)$, and the sum of
squared singular values is the squared Frobenius norm. The second expands the
square, $\norm{\Pi-\hat\Pi}_F^2=\tr\Pi+\tr\hat\Pi-2\tr(\Pi\hat\Pi)=2r-2\sum\cos^2\phi_i$.
The third, that the operator-norm distance between the projectors is the sine of
the \emph{largest} angle, is a standard result \cite{stewartsun1990,golubvanloan2013}. The
programme summarizes the list of angles by one number.

\begin{definition}[Subspace overlap]\label{def:ch13:overlap}
For $r$-dimensional subspaces with projectors $\Pi_R$ and $\Pi_{\hat R}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:overlap}
o(\mathcal R,\hat{\mathcal R})=\frac1r\tr\big(\Pi_R\Pi_{\hat R}\big)=\frac1r\sum_{i=1}^r\cos^2\phi_i\ \in[0,1].
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The overlap is $1$ when the subspaces coincide and $0$ when they are orthogonal.
It is an average of squared cosines. It is not the cosine of an average angle,
and it does not say which directions were missed. A value of $o$ cannot be
read without a reference value. That reference is what a subspace chosen at
random would score.

Let $\hat{\mathcal R}$ be uniformly random, for example the span of $r$
independent standard Gaussian vectors. Its distribution does not change when
it is rotated, so the matrix $\E[\Pi_{\hat R}]$ satisfies
$U\,\E[\Pi_{\hat R}]\,U\T=\E[\Pi_{\hat R}]$ for every orthogonal $U$. The same
argument that \cref{ch01} uses for a randomly rotated vector shows that it is a
multiple of $I$, and its trace is $r$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:chance}
\E[\Pi_{\hat R}]=\frac rd\,I,\qquad
\E\big[o(\mathcal R,\hat{\mathcal R})\big]=\frac1r\tr\Big(\Pi_R\,\frac rd I\Big)=\frac rd .
\end{equation}
The chance overlap is $r/d$. A natural rescaling reads an overlap against it,
$(o-r/d)/(1-r/d)$, which is $0$ at the chance mean, $1$ at perfect recovery and
negative below the chance mean.

\begin{example}[An exact shared direction, barely above chance]\label{ex:ch13:angles}
In $\R^3$ let $\mathcal R$ be spanned by $e_1,e_2$ and $\hat{\mathcal R}$ by $e_1$
and $(0,3,4)\T/5$. Then
$Q\T\hat Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&3/5\end{pmatrix}$, so the principal angles have
cosines $1$ and $\tfrac35$. The first angle is $0$, a shared direction. The
second is $\arccos0.6=53.13^\circ$. The overlap is
$\tfrac12(1+\tfrac9{25})=\tfrac{17}{25}=0.68$. Chance is $\tfrac23=0.667$, so
this pair, which shares a whole direction exactly, is only just above what a
random plane scores, and the rescaled value is $(0.68-\tfrac23)/(1-\tfrac23)=0.04$.
The identities \cref{eq:ch13:projid} give $\norm{\Pi-\hat\Pi}_F^2=2\cdot\tfrac{16}{25}=\tfrac{32}{25}$
and $\norm{\Pi-\hat\Pi}_2=\tfrac45=\sin53.13^\circ$. In $\R^2$, two lines at
$60^\circ$ have overlap $\cos^260^\circ=\tfrac14$, below the chance value
$\tfrac12$. A particular estimate can do worse than a random one.
\end{example}

The chance value is an average. \Cref{fig:ch13:chance} shows the whole
distribution of the overlap between a fixed $4$-dimensional subspace and a
random one, in $d=8$, $16$ and $32$ dimensions. Each histogram is centred at
$r/d$ and narrows as $d$ grows, which is concentration of measure, the subject
of \cref{ch11}. In $d=8$ the top one percent of random overlaps lies above
$0.693$. A recovered $4$-dimensional subspace of $\R^8$ with overlap $0.65$
would fail a one-percent test against chance, since about $4$ percent of random
subspaces score at least that much. The same number in $\R^{32}$ lies far above
$0.296$, the largest of the $20{,}000$ random overlaps there.

\ompnexthead{The Davis--Kahan theorem}
\section{The Davis--Kahan theorem bounds the turn of an eigenspace by the perturbation over the eigengap}\label{sec:ch13:dk}

\Cref{eq:ch13:theta} computed the turn exactly in two dimensions, and
\cref{eq:ch13:vecexp} approximated it for small perturbations. The Davis--Kahan
theorem gives a bound that needs neither, for a whole block of eigenvectors at
once. Its hypotheses matter, so they are stated in full.

Fix indices $1\le r\le s\le d$ and let $V=(v_r,\dots,v_s)$ hold the orthonormal
eigenvectors of $A$ for the eigenvalues $\lambda_r,\dots,\lambda_s$, and
$\hat V=(\hat v_r,\dots,\hat v_s)$ those of $\hat A=A+E$ for
$\hat\lambda_r,\dots,\hat\lambda_s$. The block may contain repeated eigenvalues.
The theorem bounds the principal angles between the two column spaces.

\begin{theorem}[Davis--Kahan $\sin\theta$ theorem]\label{thm:ch13:dk}
Let $\hat\lambda_0=+\infty$ and $\hat\lambda_{d+1}=-\infty$, and let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:delta}
\delta=\min\big\{\abs{\hat\lambda-\lambda}\ :\ \lambda\in[\lambda_s,\lambda_r],\ \
\hat\lambda\in(-\infty,\hat\lambda_{s+1}]\cup[\hat\lambda_{r-1},\infty)\big\}.
\end{equation}
If $\delta>0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:dk}
\norm{\sin\Theta(\hat V,V)}_F\le\frac{\norm{E}_F}{\delta},\qquad
\norm{\sin\Theta(\hat V,V)}_2\le\frac{\norm{E}_2}{\delta}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Davis and Kahan prove the bound for every unitarily invariant norm, which
includes both of these \cite{daviskahan1970}. The form above, with the
eigenvalue condition written as one number $\delta$, follows Yu, Wang and
Samworth \cite{yuwangsamworth2015}. Read the hypothesis carefully. The number
$\delta$ separates the \emph{true} eigenvalues of the block from the
\emph{perturbed} eigenvalues outside it. To use the theorem one must already know
something about the spectrum of $\hat A$. Statisticians usually know the true
spectrum, or assume it, and want a bound that uses it alone. Weyl's inequality
supplies one at the price of a factor of two.

\begin{proposition}[Population-gap form]\label{prop:ch13:dkpop}
Let $g=\min(\lambda_{r-1}-\lambda_r,\ \lambda_s-\lambda_{s+1})>0$, with
$\lambda_0=+\infty$ and $\lambda_{d+1}=-\infty$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:dkpop}
\norm{\sin\Theta(\hat V,V)}_2\le\frac{2\norm{E}_2}{g}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $\norm{E}_2\ge g/2$ the right side is at least $1$, and no sine exceeds $1$.
Otherwise Weyl's inequality gives $\hat\lambda_{r-1}\ge\lambda_{r-1}-\norm{E}_2$
and $\hat\lambda_{s+1}\le\lambda_{s+1}+\norm{E}_2$, so
$\delta\ge g-\norm{E}_2\ge g/2$. Insert this in \cref{eq:ch13:dk}.
\end{proof}

For the Frobenius norm the same idea, carried out more carefully, is the
variant of Yu, Wang and Samworth \cite{yuwangsamworth2015},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:yws}
\norm{\sin\Theta(\hat V,V)}_F\le\frac{2\min\big(\sqrt{s-r+1}\,\norm{E}_2,\ \norm{E}_F\big)}{g}.
\end{equation}
Four remarks keep the theorem honest. The gap $g$ separates the block from the
rest of the spectrum. Eigenvalues \emph{inside} the block may be equal, and the
bound is then about the subspace, never about individual eigenvectors, which are
not defined. The bounds are upper bounds and can be loose. They become vacuous
once $\norm{E}_2$ reaches $g/2$, which says nothing about the eigenspace, not that
it has been lost. And the conclusion is about $\sin\Theta$, so through
\cref{eq:ch13:projid} it bounds the projector distance $\norm{\Pi-\hat\Pi}$ and the
overlap deficit $1-o$.

\begin{example}[A repeated eigenvalue inside the block]\label{ex:ch13:dk}
Let $A=\diag(9,9,1)$ and $E=3(e_1e_3\T+e_3e_1\T)$, so $\norm{E}_2=3$ and
$\norm{E}_F=3\sqrt2$. The top two-dimensional eigenspace of $A$ is the span of
$e_1,e_2$. Its individual eigenvectors are not defined, since $9$ is repeated,
and the eigengap to the rest is $g=9-1=8$. In $A+E$ the coordinates $1$ and $3$
form the block $\begin{pmatrix}9&3\\3&1\end{pmatrix}$, with trace $10$ and
determinant $0$, so eigenvalues $10$ and $0$ and top eigenvector
$(3,1)\T/\sqrt{10}$. The eigenvalues of $A+E$ are $10$, $9$, $0$, each within
$3$ of $9,9,1$. Its top eigenspace is spanned by $e_2$ and $(3,0,1)\T/\sqrt{10}$,
and the principal cosines against $\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)$ are $1$ and
$3/\sqrt{10}$. So $\sin^2\phi_2=0.1$, $\norm{\sin\Theta}_2=\norm{\sin\Theta}_F=0.3162$
and $\phi_2=18.43^\circ$, which also follows from $\tan2\phi_2=6/8$ in
\cref{eq:ch13:rot}. The overlap is $0.95$.

The bounds rank as follows. In \cref{thm:ch13:dk} the block's true eigenvalues
are $\{9\}$ and the nearest perturbed eigenvalue outside is $\hat\lambda_3=0$, so
$\delta=9$ and the operator bound is $3/9=0.333$, close to the truth $0.316$. The
Frobenius bound is $3\sqrt2/9=0.471$. The population-gap bound
\cref{eq:ch13:dkpop} is $2\cdot3/8=0.75$, and \cref{eq:ch13:yws} gives
$2\cdot3\sqrt2/8=1.06$, which says nothing. Knowing the perturbed spectrum buys
a sharper bound. Not knowing it costs the factor of two and more.
\end{example}

\Cref{fig:ch13:dk} returns to the $2\times2$ family of \cref{eq:ch13:rot}. Since
the angle depends only on $x=\varepsilon/g$, every gap collapses onto one curve.
For this family $\delta=g/2+\sqrt{g^2/4+\varepsilon^2}$, so \cref{eq:ch13:dk}
reads $\sin\theta\le x/(\tfrac12+\sqrt{\tfrac14+x^2})$. That bound follows the
exact curve closely for small $x$ and stays below $1$. The population-gap bound
$2x$ is twice as steep and is vacuous from $x=\tfrac12$ on.

\ompnexthead{Sample covariance eigenvectors}
\section{A sample covariance's eigenvectors approach the true ones at the rate of the sampling noise over the eigengap}\label{sec:ch13:sample}

The perturbation that matters most in practice is estimation error. Draw
$x_1,\dots,x_n$ independently from $\Normal(0,\Sigma)$, with the mean known to be
$0$ to keep the algebra short, and form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:samplecov}
\hat\Sigma=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^nx_kx_k\T,\qquad \E[\hat\Sigma]=\Sigma,\qquad E=\hat\Sigma-\Sigma .
\end{equation}
The estimate is unbiased, but every entry fluctuates. By Isserlis' theorem for
Gaussian fourth moments \cite{isserlis1918},
$\E[x_ix_jx_kx_l]=\Sigma_{ij}\Sigma_{kl}+\Sigma_{ik}\Sigma_{jl}+\Sigma_{il}\Sigma_{jk}$, so
$\Var(x_ix_j)=\Sigma_{ii}\Sigma_{jj}+\Sigma_{ij}^2$. Averaging $n$ independent copies
divides that by $n$, and summing over all entries gives the size of the whole
perturbation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:frob}
\E\norm{\hat\Sigma-\Sigma}_F^2=\frac{(\tr\Sigma)^2+\tr(\Sigma^2)}{n}.
\end{equation}
Combined with \cref{eq:ch13:yws} and Jensen's inequality
$\E\norm{E}_F\le(\E\norm{E}_F^2)^{1/2}$, the top-$r$ eigenspace of $\hat\Sigma$ obeys
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:samplerate}
\E\norm{\sin\Theta}_F\le\frac{2}{g}\sqrt{\frac{(\tr\Sigma)^2+\tr(\Sigma^2)}{n}} .
\end{equation}
The rate in $n$ is $n^{-1/2}$ and the constant is set by the eigengap. The bound is
crude. Its numerator grows like $d$ for a covariance near the identity, while the
error that actually reaches the top eigenvector is much smaller. The first-order
formula \cref{eq:ch13:vecexp} shows why. In the eigenbasis of $\Sigma$ the
coordinates are independent with variances $\lambda_j$, so the coupling
$v_j\T Ev_1=\tfrac1n\sum_k(v_j\T x_k)(v_1\T x_k)$ has mean $0$ and variance
$\lambda_j\lambda_1/n$. Each direction contributes its own noise over its own gap.
To first order in $1/n$, which is accurate once every term below is small and
$\lambda_1$ is simple,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:samplefo}
\E\sin^2\theta_1\ \approx\ \frac1n\sum_{j\ge2}\frac{\lambda_1\lambda_j}{(\lambda_1-\lambda_j)^2}.
\end{equation}

The eigenvalues have a bias of their own. The largest eigenvalue
$\lambda_1(S)=\max_{\norm u=1}u\T Su$ is a maximum of linear functions of $S$ and
so is convex. Jensen's inequality then gives
$\E[\lambda_1(\hat\Sigma)]\ge\lambda_1(\E\hat\Sigma)=\lambda_1(\Sigma)$. On
average the top sample eigenvalue overestimates the top population eigenvalue,
by an amount that shrinks with $n$. A single sample can still fall below it. This is not in conflict with Weyl's inequality, which bounds
the error by $\norm E_2$ and says nothing about its sign.

\begin{example}[A spiked covariance in twenty dimensions]\label{ex:ch13:sample}
Let $d=20$ and $\Sigma=\diag(1+g,1,\dots,1)$. For $g=2$ the numerator of
\cref{eq:ch13:frob} is $(\tr\Sigma)^2+\tr\Sigma^2=22^2+(9+19)=512$, and
\cref{eq:ch13:samplerate} gives $\E\sin\theta_1\le\sqrt{512/n}$, which is $0.800$
at $n=800$ and $0.566$ at $n=1600$. The approximation \cref{eq:ch13:samplefo} has
$19$ equal terms $\lambda_1\lambda_j/g^2=3/4$, so
$\sin\theta_1\approx\sqrt{19\cdot3/n}/2$, which is $0.0944$ at $n=1600$ and
$0.0667$ at $n=3200$. The simulation of \cref{fig:ch13:sample}, $200$ seeded
repetitions per point, measures mean angles $0.0935$ and $0.0661$. For $g=0.5$
the approximation gives $\sqrt{19\cdot1.5/3200}/0.5=0.1887$ at $n=3200$, and the
simulation gives $0.1852$. The ratio between the two gaps is $2.80$ in the
simulation and $(\sqrt{1.5}/0.5)/(\sqrt3/2)=2.83$ by \cref{eq:ch13:samplefo}, not
the ratio $4$ of the gaps, because the larger spike also carries more noise. At
$n=25$ the mean top sample eigenvalue is $3.32$ when $\lambda_1=1.5$ and $4.24$
when $\lambda_1=3$, both well above the truth.
\end{example}

\ompnexthead{Sample spectra in high dimension}
\section{In high dimension a sample covariance's eigenvalues spread out even when the truth is isotropic}\label{sec:ch13:highdim}

\Cref{sec:ch13:sample} held $d$ fixed and let $n$ grow. Data in the programme
often has $d$ in the hundreds or thousands and $n$ not much larger. Then even the
eigenvalues, the stable half of the story, look wrong in a systematic way.

Take $\Sigma=I$, so every population eigenvalue is $1$ and every direction is an
eigenvector. Setting $\Sigma=I$ in \cref{eq:ch13:frob} gives
$\E\norm{\hat\Sigma-I}_F^2=(d^2+d)/n$. The cross term in
$\norm{\hat\Sigma}_F^2=\norm{I}_F^2+2\tr(\hat\Sigma-I)+\norm{\hat\Sigma-I}_F^2$ has mean
$0$, so the first two moments of the $d$ sample eigenvalues $\hat\lambda_i$ are exact,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:spread}
\E\Big[\frac1d\sum_i\hat\lambda_i\Big]=1,\qquad
\E\Big[\frac1d\sum_i\hat\lambda_i^2\Big]=1+\frac{d+1}{n}.
\end{equation}
The sample eigenvalues average to the truth, but their spread around it is
about $(d+1)/n$ in variance. It vanishes only when $n\gg d$. With $d/n$ fixed, the
sample spectrum stays spread however much data arrives.

The limiting shape of that spread is a classical result. As $d$ and $n$ grow with
$d/n\to\gamma\in(0,1]$, the histogram of the eigenvalues of $\hat\Sigma$ converges to
the Marchenko--Pastur law \cite{marchenkopastur1967}, with density
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:mp}
f_\gamma(x)=\frac{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{2\pi\gamma x}\ \ \text{on }[a,b],\qquad
a=(1-\sqrt\gamma)^2,\qquad b=(1+\sqrt\gamma)^2 .
\end{equation}
Its mean is $1$ and its variance is $\gamma$, matching \cref{eq:ch13:spread} to
first order. This book uses the law only as a named fact. Its proof needs
tools that are not developed here.

\begin{example}[Two hundred dimensions, eight hundred samples]\label{ex:ch13:mp}
With $d=200$ and $n=800$, $\gamma=\tfrac14$, so the law predicts eigenvalues
between $a=(1-\tfrac12)^2=0.25$ and $b=(1+\tfrac12)^2=2.25$, and
\cref{eq:ch13:spread} predicts a mean square of $1+201/800=1.2513$. One seeded
draw, shown in \cref{fig:ch13:mp}, has smallest eigenvalue $0.265$, largest
$2.297$, mean $1.011$ and mean square $1.280$. Averaged over $100$ draws the mean
square is within $0.3$ percent of $1.2513$. The sample covariance has condition
number $8.66$, although the true covariance has condition number $1$.
\end{example}

\ompnexthead{The power method}
\section{The power method converges at the ratio of the two largest eigenvalues}\label{sec:ch13:power}

The rest of the chapter is about computing spectra. Primer~L, section~L.7,
introduces power iteration on the matrix with rows $(2,1)$ and $(1,2)$. Here it
gets its convergence rate, which is another appearance of the eigengap.

\begin{theorem}[Rate of the power method]\label{thm:ch13:power}
Let $A$ be symmetric with $\abs{\lambda_1}>\abs{\lambda_2}\ge\dots\ge\abs{\lambda_d}$
(eigenvalues ordered by magnitude here), and let $x_0$ have $c_1=v_1\T x_0\ne0$. The
iterates $x_k=A^kx_0/\norm{A^kx_0}$ make an angle $\theta_k$ with $v_1$ that satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:power}
\tan\theta_k\le\Big(\frac{\abs{\lambda_2}}{\abs{\lambda_1}}\Big)^k\tan\theta_0 .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $x_0=\sum_jc_jv_j$. Then $A^kx_0=\sum_jc_j\lambda_j^kv_j$, and
$\tan\theta_k$ is the length of the part outside $v_1$ over the length along it,
\[
\tan\theta_k=\frac{\big(\sum_{j\ge2}c_j^2\lambda_j^{2k}\big)^{1/2}}{\abs{c_1}\abs{\lambda_1}^k}
\le\Big(\frac{\abs{\lambda_2}}{\abs{\lambda_1}}\Big)^k\frac{\big(\sum_{j\ge2}c_j^2\big)^{1/2}}{\abs{c_1}} .\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

The eigenvalue estimate converges faster. For $\lambda_1>0$ the largest
eigenvalue, the Rayleigh quotient of the iterate satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:rq}
\lambda_1-x_k\T Ax_k=\frac{\sum_{j\ge2}(\lambda_1-\lambda_j)c_j^2\lambda_j^{2k}}{\sum_jc_j^2\lambda_j^{2k}}
\le(\lambda_1-\lambda_d)\tan^2\theta_k ,
\end{equation}
so its error falls with the \emph{square} of the ratio per step. By
\cref{eq:ch13:power}, the number of steps that reduces $\tan\theta$ by a factor
$10^{-p}$ is at most $p\ln10/\ln(\abs{\lambda_1}/\abs{\lambda_2})$, rounded up.
The bound is attained when the start has no component beyond $v_1$ and $v_2$.
A ratio of $0.9$ then needs $132$ steps for $p=6$, and a ratio of $0.99$ needs $1375$.
A small relative eigengap makes the power method slow, as it made the eigenvector
sensitive in \cref{sec:ch13:turn}.

\begin{example}[Primer L's iteration, with its rate]\label{ex:ch13:power}
For $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, with $\lambda_1=3$, $\lambda_2=1$,
$v_1=(1,1)\T/\sqrt2$ and $v_2=(1,-1)\T/\sqrt2$, the start $e_1$ has equal
components along $v_1$ and $v_2$, so $\tan\theta_0=1$. Exactly,
$A^ke_1=\tfrac12\big(3^k(1,1)\T+(1,-1)\T\big)$, which gives Primer~L's iterates
$(2,1)$, $(5,4)$ and $(14,13)$. Their angles to $v_1$ have tangents
$\tfrac13$, $\tfrac19$ and $\tfrac1{27}$, so \cref{eq:ch13:power} holds with
equality. The Rayleigh quotient error is $2/(3^{2k}+1)$, which is
$2/730=0.00274$ at $k=3$, while the angle there still has tangent $0.037$.
\end{example}

The theorem needs a gap between $\abs{\lambda_1}$ and $\abs{\lambda_2}$. Blum,
Hopcroft and Kannan point out that the method is useful without one
\cite{blumhopcroftkannan2020}, provided the goal is a direction with a large
Rayleigh quotient rather than a particular eigenvector. For positive semidefinite
$A$, let $W$ be the span of the eigenvectors with $\lambda_j\ge(1-\epsilon)\lambda_1$
and let the unit start $x_0$ have $\abs{v_1\T x_0}\ge\eta>0$. The proof of \cref{thm:ch13:power}, with the
sum split at $W$ instead of at $v_1$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:gapfree}
\norm{(I-\Pi_W)x_k}\le\frac{(1-\epsilon)^k}{\eta}\le\frac{e^{-\epsilon k}}{\eta}.
\end{equation}
For $\epsilon=0.1$ and $\eta=0.05$ the right side drops below $0.1$ once
$k\ge\ln\big(1/(\epsilon\eta)\big)/\epsilon$, that is after $53$ steps, however close
$\lambda_2$ is to $\lambda_1$. A random Gaussian start has $\eta$ of order
$1/\sqrt d$ with probability bounded away from zero, as Blum, Hopcroft and Kannan
show, and \cref{ch11} explains why random directions behave this way.

To find the second eigenvector, \emph{deflate}. Once $\lambda_1$ and $v_1$ are known,
the matrix $A-\lambda_1v_1v_1\T$ has the same eigenvectors with $\lambda_1$ replaced
by $0$, and when $\abs{\lambda_2}>\abs{\lambda_3}$ the power method applied to it
converges to $v_2$ at the rate $\abs{\lambda_3}/\abs{\lambda_2}$. Errors propagate. If the computed $\hat v_1$ is off by
an angle $\theta$, the deflated matrix differs from the exact one by
$\lambda_1(v_1v_1\T-\hat v_1\hat v_1\T)$, whose operator norm is $\lambda_1\sin\theta$
by \cref{eq:ch13:projid}, and Weyl's inequality then allows each later eigenvalue
to be off by that much. Computing many eigenvectors one at a time this way
accumulates error, which is why practical codes iterate on a block. Subspace
iteration repeats $X\leftarrow\operatorname{orth}(AX)$ on a $d\times r$ block and,
when $\abs{\lambda_r}>\abs{\lambda_{r+1}}$ and the starting block is not deficient
in the top-$r$ eigenspace, converges to it at the rate $\abs{\lambda_{r+1}}/\abs{\lambda_r}$
\cite{golubvanloan2013}.

\begin{example}[Deflation in three dimensions]\label{ex:ch13:deflate}
The matrix $A$ with rows $(3,1,0)$, $(1,3,0)$, $(0,0,1)$ has eigenvalues $4,2,1$
and top eigenvector $v_1=(1,1,0)\T/\sqrt2$. Deflating gives
\[
B=A-4v_1v_1\T=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},
\]
with eigenvalues $2,1,0$. From $x_0=(1,0,1)\T$ the iterates are
$Bx_0=(1,-1,1)\T$, $B^2x_0=(2,-2,1)\T$ and in general
$B^kx_0=(2^{k-1},-2^{k-1},1)\T$, which turns toward $v_2=(1,-1,0)\T/\sqrt2$ with
$\tan\theta_k=1/(\sqrt2\,2^{k-1})$, halving each step as the ratio $\tfrac12$ predicts.
\end{example}

evenly from $0.6\rho$ down to $0$, from one random start. After a few steps,
during which the small eigenvalues die out quickly, each curve settles onto the
slope $\log_{10}\rho$ of \cref{eq:ch13:power}. The fitted slopes agree with

\ompnexthead{Krylov and randomized methods}
\section{For large matrices, Krylov methods and random sketches find the top of the spectrum from a few products}\label{sec:ch13:large}

A dense eigendecomposition of a $d\times d$ matrix costs on the order of $d^3$
operations. For a covariance of a few hundred dimensions that is nothing. For a
graph Laplacian on $32{,}000$ points, or a matrix that is only available as a
routine computing $x\mapsto Ax$, it is out of reach. Two families of methods
avoid it. This section gives the intuition only. Golub and Van Loan
\cite{golubvanloan2013} and Halko, Martinsson and Tropp \cite{halkomartinssontropp2011}
give the analysis.

The power method throws information away. After $k$ steps it keeps only $A^kx_0$,
although it has computed $x_0,Ax_0,\dots,A^{k-1}x_0$ along the way. The span of
those vectors is the \emph{Krylov subspace}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:krylov}
\mathcal K_k(A,x_0)=\operatorname{span}\{x_0,Ax_0,\dots,A^{k-1}x_0\}=\{p(A)x_0:\ p\ \text{a polynomial of degree}<k\}.
\end{equation}
The Lanczos method builds an orthonormal basis $Q_k$ of $\mathcal K_k$ one vector at
a time, using only products with $A$, and takes the eigenvalues of the small
$k\times k$ matrix $Q_k\T AQ_k$ as approximations, called \emph{Ritz values}. The
power method's iterate is one element of the Krylov subspace, $p(A)x_0$ with
$p(t)=t^{k-1}$. Lanczos picks the best element, so in exact arithmetic its top
Ritz value is never worse than the power method's Rayleigh quotient after the
same number of products, and with a small relative gap it is usually far better, because a
well-chosen polynomial separates $\lambda_1$ from $\lambda_2$ much faster than
$t^{k-1}$ does \cite{golubvanloan2013}. In floating point the Lanczos vectors lose
orthogonality, and production codes add reorthogonalization or restarts.

\begin{example}[One product, two answers]\label{ex:ch13:krylov}
Let $A=\diag(3,2,1)$ and $x_0=(1,1,1)\T$. One product gives $Ax_0=(3,2,1)\T$. The
power method reports its Rayleigh quotient $(27+8+1)/(9+4+1)=36/14=2.5714$. The
Krylov subspace $\mathcal K_2$ is spanned by $(1,1,1)\T$ and $(3,2,1)\T$, or
equally by $(1,1,1)\T$ and $(1,0,-1)\T$. With $K$ holding those two vectors,
$K\T K=\diag(3,2)$ and $K\T AK=\begin{pmatrix}6&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$, so the Ritz
values solve $\det(K\T AK-\mu K\T K)=6\mu^2-24\mu+20=0$, a pencil of the kind
\cref{ch01} treats. The larger root is $2+\sqrt6/3=2.8165$. A second power step,
$A^2x_0=(9,4,1)\T$, reaches only $276/98=2.8163$. One product used well beats
two products used the power method's way.
\end{example}

Randomized methods take a different route to the same place. To approximate the
top-$k$ part of an $m\times n$ matrix $A$, multiply it by a random Gaussian
matrix $\Omega$ with $k+p$ columns, where $p$ is a small oversampling such as $5$
or $10$, and orthonormalize,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch13:rsvd}
Y=(AA\T)^qA\,\Omega,\qquad Y=QR,\qquad B=Q\T A,\qquad A\approx Q\,B=Q\,(\tilde U\Sigma_{\!s}\tilde V\T).
\end{equation}
The columns of $Y$ are random combinations of the columns of $A$, so they land
mostly in its dominant left singular subspace. The small matrix $B$ has only
$k+p$ rows and its singular value decomposition is cheap. The $q$ extra
multiplications by $AA\T$ are the power method again, applied to a whole block.
They raise the singular values to the power $2q+1$, which sharpens the
separation between the kept and the discarded part when the spectrum decays
slowly. Halko, Martinsson and Tropp prove that the expected error is close to the
best possible, $\sigma_{k+1}$ by the Eckart--Young theorem of Primer~L, section~L.8,
with a factor that depends on $k$, $p$, $q$ and the tail of the spectrum
\cite{halkomartinssontropp2011}.

\begin{example}[A sketch that is exact]\label{ex:ch13:sketch}
Let $A$ have rows $(1,1)$, $(2,2)$, $(2,2)$, a rank-one matrix
$(1,2,2)\T(1,1)$ with singular value $3\sqrt2=4.243$. A sketch with the single
column $\Omega=(1,0)\T$ gives $Y=A\Omega=(1,2,2)\T$, so $Q=(1,2,2)\T/3$, and
$QQ\T A=A$ exactly. Whenever $A$ has rank $k$ and $\Omega$ has $k$ columns in
general position, $Y$ spans the range of $A$ and the sketch loses nothing. The
randomness is there so that ``general position'' holds with probability one
without knowing $A$.
\end{example}

\Cref{fig:ch13:rsvd} measures the error of a rank-$10$ randomized approximation
of a $300\times200$ matrix, relative to the optimum $\sigma_{11}$, for two
spectra. When the singular values decay fast, as $0.7^{j-1}$, the plain sketch with
$q=0$ is already within $3$ percent of optimal. When they decay slowly, as
$j^{-1/2}$, the plain sketch is $1.79$ times the optimum, and one or two power
steps bring it to $1.09$ and then $1.02$.

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Warm-up}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:ch13:weyl}
Let $A=\diag(5,2)$ and $E=\begin{pmatrix}0&0.3\\0.3&0\end{pmatrix}$. Use Weyl's
inequality to give intervals for the eigenvalues of $A+E$, then compute them
exactly.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:ch13:lines}
Two lines through the origin of $\R^2$ meet at angle $\phi$. Write their overlap
and the operator-norm distance of their projectors in terms of $\phi$, and compare
the overlap with chance.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:ch13:projfrob}
Show that $\norm{\Pi_R-\Pi_{\hat R}}_F^2=2r(1-o)$ for $r$-dimensional subspaces with
overlap $o$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:ch13:iters}
How many power-method steps reduce $\tan\theta$ by a factor of $10^{6}$ when
$\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}=0.9$? When it is $0.99$?
\end{exercise}

\subsection*{By hand}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:ch13:second}
For $A=\diag(4,1)$ and $E=\begin{pmatrix}0&0.3\\0.3&0\end{pmatrix}$, compare the
exact top eigenvalue of $A+E$ with the first- and second-order terms of
\cref{eq:ch13:eigexp}, and the exact turn of the top eigenvector with the
first-order value $\varepsilon/g$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:ch13:dk}
For $A=\diag(2,1)$ and $E=\begin{pmatrix}0&0.5\\0.5&0\end{pmatrix}$, compute the
exact $\sin\theta$ of the top eigenvector, the Davis--Kahan bound with $\delta$
from \cref{eq:ch13:delta}, and the population-gap bound \cref{eq:ch13:dkpop}.

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:ch13:angles3}
In $\R^3$, find the principal angles, the overlap and $\norm{\Pi-\hat\Pi}_F^2$ for
$\mathcal R=\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)$ and

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:ch13:deflate}
For the deflated matrix $B$ of Example~\ref{ex:ch13:deflate} and the start
$x_0=(1,0,1)\T$, prove $B^kx_0=(2^{k-1},-2^{k-1},1)\T$ by induction and find
$\tan\theta_k$ against $v_2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:ch13:chance}
Prove $\E[\Pi_{\hat R}]=(r/d)I$ for the span $\hat{\mathcal R}$ of $r$ independent

\end{exercise}
